# Provide an AI with a map, SpectrumNet can reconstruct the entire wireless world—provided the dataset is large and comprehensive. [Includes Python code]

Generative AI meets SpectrumNet: An open benchmark for multi-band 3D radio maps
Shuhang Zhang, Shuai Jiang, Wanjie Lin, Zheng Fang, Kangjun Liu, Hongliang Zhang, Ke Chen
Abstract Radio maps are an effective tool for intuitively displaying the coverage of wireless signals in specific areas. As wireless nodes become increasingly dense and complex, they will play a more important role in future 6G networks. However, due to the extremely sparse sampling points in actual systems, it is challenging to construct high-resolution radio maps. Generative AI can create synthetic data to fill in the gaps in real measurements, precisely providing an efficient path for building high-precision radio maps. Currently, the data used to train radio map generation models are mostly limited to two-dimensional, single-band maps in urban scenarios, with poor generalization capabilities in terms of terrain, frequency bands, and height. To address this issue, we have provided a multi-band three-dimensional radio map dataset named SpectrumNet, which considers terrain and climate information. In terms of dimensions and scale, it is currently the largest radio map dataset available, containing three spatial dimensions, five frequency bands, eleven types of terrain, and map data under three climate conditions. This paper will detail the generation parameters and settings of the SpectrumNet dataset, and evaluate four baseline methods for radio map construction based on it. The experimental results prove that a dataset like SpectrumNet is essential for training models that have strong generalization capabilities in space, frequency, and terrain. Finally, the article discusses future work on SpectrumNet, including dataset expansion, calibration, and further possibilities for generating model research based on it.
Keywords Radio maps, Datasets, Generative AI, Terrain information
I. Introduction
A. The Evolution of Radio Maps
As the 6G wireless network accelerates its evolution, the increasing complexity of wireless functions and the deployment of massive numbers of wireless nodes necessitate a deeper understanding of radio information. A radio map is a set of data that reflects the characteristics of radio signals in a specific geographical area, such as signal strength and propagation environment. With it, we can intuitively understand signal coverage, quality of service, and data rate. A high-precision radio map can clearly depict the overall status of wireless propagation, thereby significantly improving spectrum efficiency and positioning accuracy. Specifically, several key applications rely on it:
1. Network Planning:
Radio maps provide information on signal strength and coverage range, helping to optimize network deployment and configuration.
2. Position Services:
Precise position tracking and localization are indispensable for navigation, augmented reality, geofencing, and other applications.
3. Emergency Communications:
In emergency situations, radio maps can guide rescue personnel to areas with good signal coverage, ensuring reliable communication.
4. Spectrum Management:
It can intuitively show the use and interference of different frequencies, helping to reduce network congestion.
Over the past two years, many researchers have explored the application of radio maps in various scenarios, from positioning and navigation to channel modeling and user identification. For example, some have proposed using deep Gaussian processes to construct indoor radio maps and estimate user positions; others have developed mobile robot platforms capable of simultaneously constructing spatial and radio maps to support indoor positioning services; and even some have utilized radio maps for drone networking to perform coverage perception navigation.
However, building high-precision radio maps faces two major challenges. First, the actual sampling rate is too low. We can only obtain precise information through the deployment of radio sensors, but due to hardware costs and deployment range limitations, the spatial sampling rate in the actual system usually does not even reach 1%. This means that more than 99% of the information cannot be directly obtained through perception. Second, the effective bandwidth of a radio map depends on the working bandwidth of the sensors. To obtain a map covering the very low frequency to Ka band, the deployment cost of the sensors will be extremely high.
Facing these challenges, generative AI is seen as a promising solution. Generative models can learn the patterns of signal propagation and RSS distribution from massive training data, which completely breaks away from the fixed paradigm of traditional channel models that can only be used in specific scenarios, significantly enhancing the generalization ability of radio map generation. A trained generative model has the potential to learn all channel propagation knowledge over all working bands. Based on sparse sampling and such a generative model, we can obtain a high-precision, large bandwidth, and high resolution radio map. The premise for training a well-performing model requires a sufficiently large radio map dataset that not only precisely describes signal propagation characteristics but also covers enough scenarios and bands.
B. Relevant research and our contributions in generating radio maps, one of the most common approaches is based on channel models. According to the general rules of signal propagation, the path loss is usually described by a variant of the lognormal shadow fading model, such as the 3GPP model. Given the position and configuration of the transmitter, a map can be generated according to the model. Some studies have adopted this approach of generating maps based on channel models and trained corresponding generative models. Although this method is simple to implement, it often deviates greatly from real scenarios when discussing wave propagation characteristics in specific environments and paths.
Compared to the ray tracing method, which provides a more accurate and realistic approach, this method simulates electromagnetic waves as rays emitted from an emitter and interacts with objects in the scene, considering reflections, even refraction and transmission. By combining multiple rays arriving at the receiver, it can calculate path loss and other information. Recently, there has been research using ray tracing to generate more precise datasets, such as RadioMapSeer, which provides five hundred thousand six-dimensional radio maps for urban scenes.
Although ray tracing is accurate, its computational load is immense due to the massive number of rays to be processed. Fortunately, in recent years, some mature commercial simulation platforms have emerged, such as WinProp and Wireless Insite, significantly improving the generation efficiency. Many scholars use these platforms to create their own datasets for training generative models for map generation. However, the problem lies in that these datasets are either not publicly available or only specific to certain scenarios (such as all urban 2D maps). Currently, preliminary studies have been conducted based on these datasets, utilizing architectures like UNet, GAN, Swin Transformer to improve map reconstruction quality.
To evaluate these generated models more fairly and accurately, a benchmark dataset covering a wider range of scenarios and dimensions is urgently needed. The existing datasets and our goal still have several barriers between them: first, all public datasets focus solely on urban scenes, ignoring rural and mountainous terrains, directly weakening the model's generalization ability for different terrains; second, existing data only considers the electromagnetic impact of objects above ground but neglects the significant role of terrain itself (altitude, material) in signal propagation; third, the existing only includes two-dimensional maps of ground networks without three-dimensional maps containing different height information, leaving a gap in integrated air, land, and space network research; finally, the frequency coverage is too narrow, making it impossible for models to generalize across the frequency dimension.
In response to these shortcomings, we have developed the largest and most comprehensive open-source radio map dataset currently available, called SpectrumNet, to support research on generation models. Additionally, based on this dataset, we have conducted some prototype experiments. The main contributions of this paper can be summarized into three points:
1  
We have released SpectrumNet, the largest open-source radio map dataset to date, comprising over 300,000 maps. It covers 11 different geographical scenarios and three climate scenarios, incorporating terrain, weather, and object material information for the first time. Additionally, it provides five different frequency band variations and is the first dataset to offer maps at three elevation levels.
2  
We conducted experiments based on SpectrumNet, training several generation models for map construction. We evaluated the structural similarity, peak signal-to-noise ratio, and root mean square error of four baseline methods under different scenarios, frequencies, and heights, which serves as a sample for subsequent research.
3  
We discussed the future expansion directions of the SpectrumNet dataset, such as how to further improve the data scale and quality. We also speculated on what new research directions this dataset might inspire in the field of generative models.
II. SpectrumNet Dataset Section, we will first introduce SpectrumNet through comparison with existing datasets and then detail its various attributes.
A. Dataset Description We have compared SpectrumNet with three recent datasets, RadioMapSeer, RadioGAT, and RMDirectionalBerlin (see Table 1).
Sample Size: SpectrumNet contains over 300,000 maps, more than four times the sample size of other publicly available datasets, and this number is expected to continue to grow in the future.
1. Spatial Dimension: SpectrumNet is the only "true-3D" radio map dataset. Although some datasets consider the three-dimensional models of buildings during modeling, the samples they ultimately produce are still two-dimensional images, incapable of being used to train non-ground network models for scenarios such as air-ground and space-ground integration.
2. Scenario Type: Existing datasets focus solely on urban scenes filled with high-rise buildings, where the model's performance drops when switching to different terrains. SpectrumNet, however, includes 11 types of terrains with diverse geographical characteristics, including dense urban areas, ordinary urban areas, rural areas, suburban areas, mountainous regions, forests, deserts, grasslands, islands, oceans, and lakes. The propagation parameters of electromagnetic waves under different materials and distributions also vary significantly.
3. Terrain Information: Besides ground objects, the terrain itself plays a crucial role in shaping signal propagation, especially in mountainous and island areas. SpectrumNet is the first to consider the impact of terrain and ground objects together, providing a more realistic channel condition model.
4. Climate Influence: Temperature, rainfall, and other climate information have a significant impact on radio wave propagation. In generating maps, SpectrumNet incorporates parameters such as atmospheric pressure, water density, temperature, and rainfall rate, offering options for tropical, subtropical, and temperate climates.
5. Frequency Diversity: To enhance the model's frequency generalization capability, SpectrumNet includes five different frequency bands, making it the only open-source dataset that supports frequency domain inference research.
6. Number of Transmitters: In complex environments, multiple transmitters can cause signal superposition, making construction more challenging, but this is exactly what the real world is like. SpectrumNet and RadioGAT are the only two datasets containing multi-transmitter radio maps.
B. Dataset Attributes
The specific parameters of the SpectrumNet dataset can be found in Table 2. We obtained a real-world map of buildings for 15,300 different areas using OpenStreetMap. Each area spans an area of (1.28km x 1.28km) on the x-y plane, with a spatial resolution of (10m), thus resulting in a generated map size of (128 x 128) pixels. SpectrumNet provides three sampling layers at different heights for each area: (1.5m), (30m), and (200m), corresponding to ground network, building height network, and airborne network respectively. In terms of frequency, we selected five bands from (150MHz) to (22GHz), essentially covering the main working frequencies of wireless communication. Additionally, different scenarios correspond to different climates, building materials, and terrain materials, which are also marked during the generation of each map.
1) Terrain Scenarios: Figure 1 displays 11 terrain scenarios within SpectrumNet.
Dense Urban Areas: Highly built-up areas with numerous reflections and refractions due to tall buildings and streets, featuring a variety of building materials, and predominantly cemented terrain.
Normal Urban Areas: Lower in density and height than dense urban areas, such as small cities or non-central areas of large cities, with buildings and terrain primarily made of cement.
Rural Areas: Mainly low-rise buildings and vast open spaces (farms), with terrain materials being soil.
Suburban Areas: Buildings typically cluster together, with a primary material being cement, and the terrain is a mixture of soil and cement.
Mountainous Areas: Significant elevation changes and significant landform uplifting, where radio propagation is mainly determined by terrain information, with the material considered to be soil.
Forest Areas: Flat terrain without buildings, covered with trees, with the main objects being wood.
Saharan Desert: A flat and open area without buildings or other objects, with terrain materials being soil.
Grassland Areas: Like the desert, but with a flatter terrain and grass instead of soil, reflecting and transmitting characteristics different from soil.
Islands: The water surface accounts for a large proportion, with significant elevation changes in the land portions. Materials vary.
Marine: Completely covered by water, without any buildings or other objects, transmission can either be direct sight paths or reflect off the water surface.
Lake: Mostly covered by water, but unlike the ocean, the terrain and buildings on the surrounding land will affect signal propagation.
2) Three-dimensional topography and building information: SpectrumNet's maps are generated based on real-world three-dimensional topography and building information. The process involves first importing the three-dimensional topographic information from OpenStreetMap (Figure 2a), then manually correcting it with OpenStreetMap and Google Maps to obtain building information (Figure 2b), and finally integrating these into a complete three-dimensional scene model (Figure 2c). Based on this three-dimensional model, we sample RSS at different heights to form a three-dimensional radio map. Figure 3 illustrates this process, with three sampling layers corresponding to the ground, buildings, and airborne networks.
Figure 4 provides an example of Shenzhen's dense urban area. Comparing the projections of buildings at different heights and their corresponding radio maps, it can be observed that the lowest layer has more buildings, yet its signal coverage is narrower than the highest layer, which is significantly obscured by more shadow effects. However, interestingly, even where there is coverage, the RSS at lower layers is actually higher because of stronger reflection effects. Additionally, in Figure 4d, the buildings projected onto high-rises are all on the right side of the transmitters, yet the left side still has shadows on the map, indicating that low-level ground buildings also block signals from high-altitude sources. This phenomenon vividly demonstrates that training integrated models for air, space, and ground communication solely with two-dimensional datasets is not feasible; three-dimensional data is essential.
3) Frequency Variation: The propagation characteristics of signals at different frequency bands are significantly different. Figure 5 demonstrates the radio maps for several frequency bands at a height of (1.5m) and shows the large-scale fading effects of the (150MHz) network and the (22GHz) network clearly. This again emphasizes that it is only by incorporating maps from multiple frequency bands into the dataset that models can be trained to have strong domain generalization capabilities in the frequency domain.
4) Weather Parameters: When constructing SpectrumNet, we refer to the ITU standards and establish weather models for over 20 cities and regions, taking into account temperature, pressure, water vapor density, liquid water density, rainfall rate, etc. Table 3 lists an example of weather parameters for the tropical city Jakarta. During map generation, we first randomly select a weather condition (e.g., heavy rain in Jakarta), then generate specific values within the corresponding parameter range randomly, and finally integrate these weather parameters' impact on propagation according to the ITU standards into the ray tracing-based map generation process.
III. Experimental Study on Radio Map Construction Based on Generative Models We ran four representative baseline methods on SpectrumNet: UNet, CBAM, Swin-unet, and interpolation algorithm. UNet, CBAM, and Swin-unet represent the mainstream path of generative models today, while the interpolation method represents traditional wireless channel-driven approaches. Each time, we randomly select 50 sample points as input for the model to complete the entire (128x128) map, with a sampling rate of approximately (0.3%). The inputs of the generative models include three channels: RSS values, terrain elevation, and building information. All experiments were completed on NVIDIA V100 GPU, and the dataset was split into training set, validation set, and test set in a ratio of 7:1:2. The evaluation metrics used were SSIM, PSNR, and RMSE.
A. Table 4 in the "Product Technology Requirements" section summarizes the results across various terrain and scene scenarios. For scenes with flat oceans and no buildings, the effect is naturally optimal; however, for dense urban areas, suburban areas, and forests, which have complex terrain or building conditions, the effect is somewhat inferior. This indicates that generative models aimed at such complex scenarios deserve more research effort. From a methodological standpoint, CBAM performs best in most scenarios, proving the value of channel attention and spatial attention mechanisms in this type of task. The interpolation algorithm's performance is the worst.
As an example, Figures 6 and 7 respectively show the reconstruction under dense urban areas and mountainous scenes. In dense urban areas, the effects of buildings are highly obstructive, while in mountainous scenes, the terrain's undulations dominate, showing that CBAM generally gets it right but still lacks detail fidelity.
B. Table 5 in the "3D Radio Map Construction Table" provides results at different heights. It's evident that the (200m) altitude yields the best results for air network reconstruction, while the (1.5m) ground network reconstruction is the worst. This makes sense because the ground network is significantly more complex due to the strong impact of buildings and terrain obstruction and reflection compared to the cleaner, higher-altitude scenes. Methodologically, Swin-unet consistently ranks first across most heights, indicating its pooling and attention mechanisms are adept at feature selection. The interpolation algorithm remains at the bottom.
Figure 8 is an example of ground network reconstruction, where numerous details are visible, making baseline methods challenging to accurately reconstruct. This area indeed warrants further research. Figure 9 presents a more interesting example of air network reconstruction. Although the (200m) altitude itself does not have any buildings, noticeable shadow effects can still be seen on the radio map. The reason is simple: the transmitter is still on the ground, and even low buildings can block signals directed upwards. This phenomenon emphasizes that to solve such problems, incorporating three-dimensional information into generative models is essential, as relying solely on two-dimensional data is insufficient.
C. Multi-Frequency Radio Map Construction Table 6 presents the performance across different frequency bands. The construction of maps for high-frequency networks, especially in terms of SSIM metrics, is significantly more challenging than that of low-frequency networks. This is because high-frequency signals are more easily attenuated by terrain and buildings, making it naturally more difficult to accurately reproduce in complex environments. Therefore, special attention should be given to developing models capable of handling the generation of maps for high-frequency bands. In terms of methods, CBAM performs best across most bands, followed by UNet, with Swin-unet and interpolation algorithms performing more generally.
Figures 10 and 11 are combined for a visual comparison. It is very intuitive to see that (22GHz) high-frequency networks are significantly more affected by terrain and building obstructions than (150MHz) low-frequency networks. High-frequency network maps are filled with fine features caused by subtle topographic variations and small objects. Figures 10 and 11 can explain this phenomenon clearly.
D. Generalization Discussion 1) Cross-Terrain Generalization: To verify the model's ability to generalize across different terrains, we trained three sets of UNet, CBAM, and Swin-unet models using data from "lakes", "dense urban areas", and "mountains" respectively, and then let them generate maps for all these scenarios. The results are listed in Tables 7, 8, and 9.
Overall, models trained with data identical to the test scenario performed best. Models trained with complex scenarios such as "dense urban areas" or "mountains" showed significantly stronger generalization abilities to other scenarios than those trained with simple scenarios like "lakes". Figures 12 and 13 provide comparative results for the same model generating maps in dense urban areas. The map generated by the "mountain" model shows completely different signal distributions from reality; whereas the map generated by the model trained on "dense urban areas" at least has a rough approximation of the actual situation, though the detail accuracy is still lacking. Therefore, to enhance the model's generalization capability, it is necessary to include a sufficient amount of data from complex scenarios in the training set.
2) Cross-Frequency Generalization: We have also verified the cross-frequency generalization ability using a similar method. The models were trained with (150MHz) and (22GHz) data, and then used to generate maps for five frequency bands, as shown in Tables 10 and 11. The conclusion is straightforward: the model trained with (22GHz) high-frequency data performs better when generating maps of high-frequency networks; the same holds true in reverse. To achieve good performance across all frequency bands, the training set must include data from multiple frequency bands. Additionally, we found that the model learned on (150MHz) could generate maps for (1.5GHz), (1.7GHz), and (3.5GHz) fairly well, but it completely failed when generating maps for (22GHz). Conversely, the model trained with (22GHz) data performed relatively well across all five frequency bands. This demonstrates that the various impacts of minor terrain and buildings on map generation in high-frequency bands are crucial for the model's cross-frequency generalization. Figures 14 and 15 illustrate this difference well: models trained with low-frequency data can only capture large-scale fading effects, completely ignoring terrain details; whereas models truly trained with high-frequency data can clearly depict the subtle impacts of terrain on propagation.
E. Complexity Analysis We also looked at the differences in computational complexity, parameter count, and inference time among these four methods. Swin-Unet has significantly higher complexity due to its complex pooling and attention mechanisms compared to other methods; interpolation algorithms, by contrast, do not involve neural networks and naturally have the lowest complexity. CBAM has a slightly higher complexity than UNet, but given its previous performance advantages, it can be considered the most efficient baseline method in most cases.
IV. Discussion and Future Work Outlook A. Data Set Expansion SpectrumNet currently has much room for improvement:
1.
Spatial Expansion: The current area size is (1.28km x 1.28km), which may not be sufficient for some large-radius communities. Future expansion will be to (5km x 5km) while maintaining the same resolution.
2  
Full-band construction: In terms of frequency dimensions, the current resolution at these five points is still too coarse. We will improve the resolution in the future and cover all commonly used wireless network bands.
3  
Time-domain construction: In the real world, radio maps are time-correlated at the transmitter location, power, and weather conditions. However, all existing data ignore this correlation. Future versions will incorporate multiple maps in continuous time slots, which can be used to train models to predict moving transmitter trajectories, power variations, and even weather changes, enabling applications such as channel prediction.
4  
Directional propagation: Now, all antennas are omnidirectional by default. However, multi-antenna and beamforming are becoming increasingly widespread, and future versions will surely incorporate scenarios that reflect the anisotropic distribution of signals under directional transmission.
5  
Data set calibration: Currently, all simulation data produced using ray tracing has not been validated against reality. We plan to add a hardware experiment phase in the future, using real-collected data to analyze whether the simulations are accurate and can also be used to calibrate the reflectivity and refraction coefficients of different materials.
B. Extension research of generative models with the SpectrumNet open-source dataset can inspire many new research directions:
1  
Model Enhancement:
Expand the model scale:
The dimension and scale of the dataset have increased, making the knowledge more complex. The old models are no longer sufficient, we must move to a stronger model.
Specially designed model framework:
1. Developing a new framework that integrates topography, architecture, and propagation laws is essential for radio map reconstruction.
2. Integrating channel models as preprocessing techniques can help streamline input data or incorporate physical laws into the loss function to facilitate faster and more accurate learning of electromagnetic wave propagation patterns.
3. SpectrumNet pioneered the integration of spatial, frequency, topography, and weather dimensions, laying the groundwork for innovative applications such as training cross-space-frequency models.
4. The development of an open-source multi-band three-dimensional radio map dataset, SpectrumNet, incorporating terrain and climate information, provides a comprehensive view of how these factors are integrated into map construction.
5. Our experiments based on SpectrumNet demonstrate the necessity of this dataset for training models with strong generalization capabilities.
6. The main conclusions include:
   1. The need for a novel framework that integrates topography, architecture, and propagation laws for radio map reconstruction.
   2. The utility of integrating channel models as preprocessing techniques to streamline input data or incorporate physical laws into the loss function to facilitate faster and more accurate learning of electromagnetic wave propagation patterns.
   3. The importance of SpectrumNet in pioneering the integration of spatial, frequency, topography, and weather dimensions, laying the groundwork for innovative applications such as training cross-space-frequency models.
SpectrumNet is currently the largest radio map dataset in terms of dimensions and scale. It is three-dimensional, covering five frequency bands, eleven terrain types, and three climates. The generation of each image has fully considered the physical characteristics such as reflection and refraction of signals under different terrains, climates, and material properties of objects.
2 
We evaluated four methods: UNet, CBAM, Swin-unet, and interpolation algorithms using SpectrumNet. The results show that models trained with complex data such as dense urban areas, ground networks, and high-frequency networks perform better when generalizing to other scenarios, heights, and frequencies. This also indirectly demonstrates that previous small datasets with single dimensions are insufficient for training models with generalization capabilities.
3 
We discussed future directions for expansion, including how to expand the dimensionality of the dataset further and how to use real data for calibration. Based on SpectrumNet, there are many new directions we can explore, such as designing more powerful model frameworks and cross-domain model training.
